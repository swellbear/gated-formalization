## A. Process / identity (descriptive)

| ID | Claim |
|----|--------|
| A1 | This release is the Summary of Economic Projections in conjunction with the June 16–17, 2026 FOMC meeting. |
| A2 | It was released at 2:00 p.m. EDT, June 17, 2026. |
| A3 | Meeting participants submitted projections of the **most likely outcomes** for real GDP growth, the unemployment rate, and inflation for each year 2026–2028 and over the longer run. |
| A4 | Each participant’s projections were based on information available at the time of the meeting, plus that participant’s assessment of **appropriate monetary policy** (including a funds-rate path and its longer-run value) and assumptions about other factors likely to affect outcomes. |
| A5 | **“Appropriate monetary policy”** is defined as the future path of policy that each participant deems most likely to foster outcomes for activity and inflation that best satisfy **his or her individual interpretation** of the statutory mandate (maximum employment and price stability). |
| A6 | **Longer-run** projections are each participant’s assessment of the value to which each variable would be expected to **converge, over time, under appropriate monetary policy and in the absence of further shocks**. |
| A7 | Nineteen participants submitted in conjunction with the **March 17–18, 2026** meeting. |
| A8 | **Eighteen** participants submitted in conjunction with the June 16–17, 2026 meeting; **one of those 18 did not submit 2028** projections. |
| A9 | March projections shown in the tables were made in conjunction with the March 17–18, 2026 meeting. |
| A10 | The economic projections “inform discussions of monetary policy among policymakers and can aid public understanding of the basis for policy actions.” |
| A11 | “Considerable uncertainty attends these projections.” |
| A12 | Models used to help produce forecasts are “necessarily imperfect descriptions of the real world,” and the future path of the economy can be affected by “myriad unforeseen developments and events.” |
| A13 | In setting policy, participants consider not only the most likely outcome embodied in their projections but also alternative possibilities, their likelihood, and potential costs should they occur. |

## B. Measurement / definition (descriptive)

| ID | Claim |
|----|--------|
| B1 | GDP and both inflation measures are **Q4/Q4** percent changes. |
| B2 | PCE inflation = percent change in the PCE price index; core PCE = PCE excluding food and energy. |
| B3 | Unemployment rate projections are the **average civilian unemployment rate in Q4** of the indicated year. |
| B4 | Federal funds rate projections are the **midpoint of the projected appropriate target range** or the projected **appropriate target level**, at **year-end** (or longer run). |
| B5 | For each period, the **median** is the middle projection (average of the two middle when even). |
| B6 | **Central tendency** excludes the three highest and three lowest projections for each variable in each year. |
| B7 | **Range** is lowest to highest participant projection for that variable/year. |
| B8 | Longer-run projections for **core PCE are not collected**. |
| B9 | Figure 1 actuals are **annual** data. |
| B10 | Figure 2 dots are rounded to the nearest **1/8** percentage point. |
| B11 | Table 2 consumer-price RMSE uses **CPI** (not PCE), Q4/Q4. |
| B12 | Table 2 short-rate errors: Fed staff = federal funds rate; other forecasts = 3-month Treasury bills; errors on **Q4 average levels**. |
| B13 | Table 2 error ranges are ± RMSE of summer projections for **2006–2025** by various private and government forecasters. |
| B14 | Under stated assumptions, those error ranges correspond to about a **70 percent** probability that actual outcomes lie in the implied ranges. |
| B15 | Funds-rate fan-chart CIs are **not strictly consistent** with SEP funds-rate projections, because those projections are **not** forecasts of the likeliest funds-rate outcomes but assessments of **appropriate policy**, on an end-of-year basis. |
| B16 | Dispersion of projections **across participants** (Figure 1) is **much smaller** than average forecast errors over the past 20 years (Figures 4.A–4.C). |
