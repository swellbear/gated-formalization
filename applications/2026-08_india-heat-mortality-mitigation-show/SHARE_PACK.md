## 1. Plain verdict

The paper **reports** a model in which national heat-death paths, driven by summer temperatures, rise faster and look more uneven across states under a high-emission climate path than under an intermediate path.

**That does not demonstrate that climate mitigation substantially reduces urban heat-health burdens.** The run uses two climate **scenarios**, not a real mitigation law, and **national** death counts plus city **temperatures**, not city death counts. The authors’ own limits section already lists missing humidity, coarse grids, and no city-level deaths. Their conclusion says mitigation **could** reduce the burden — weaker than the abstract’s **demonstrate**.

**Bar height:** the abstract treats the finding as **shown** (not merely possible). That bar is **not established**.

**Reliability of this scoring pass:** medium — single-operator reading of the public HTML; no second scorer; no independent climate rerun (stand-in).

**Live vs stand-in:** **Live** for what [arXiv:2603.24244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24244) prints (retrieved 2026-08-13). **Stand-in** for future deaths, city deaths, and real mitigation policy.

## 2. Established

- What this document says it did (national 1970–2023 heat-death compilation; ARIMAX; six-model SMT; 67 ~1° sites; two SSPs)  
- That it **prints** a steeper, more spread-out high-emission path in that setup  
- That the authors flag absolute counts as uncertain and list major limits  

## Not established

- That climate **mitigation** is **shown** to cut both the **size** and **unevenness** of future **urban** heat-health burdens  
- City-level mortality  
- A real policy evaluation  
- Independent confirmation of their numbers
